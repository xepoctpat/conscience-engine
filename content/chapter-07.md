# Chapter 07 — The Ledger

The project finally received the name it had been resisting.

Not SIR.

The Ledger.

By then the promise was simple enough to be dangerous: a decision could be followed forward until its cost appeared somewhere with a name, a route, or a locked door.

Elias wrote accountability in the design notes and then spent twenty minutes trying to improve the word. He failed. Some words were ugly because they had work to do.

People could choose any action they wished, after argument, appeal, delay, anger, and all the ordinary weather of democracy.

They simply could not pretend afterward that the cost had arrived from nowhere.

They also added a rule Anika insisted on writing herself: no person could be reduced to absence. Missing data had to remain visible as missing, not silently converted into zero, average, or acceptable uncertainty. Kofi called this basic manners. SIR called it epistemic humility. Mrs. Pelt called it about time.

The Ledger’s first public interface was therefore less elegant than Elias wanted and more truthful than most people expected. Beside every projection sat its known absences, challenge paths, affected groups, reversibility estimate, and a plain-language sentence beginning: If this decision is made, these people are likely to carry its cost.

Elias wanted the interface to open with the clean map. Anika moved the absences to the top. Kofi changed the button from accept projection to acknowledge reading. Mrs. Pelt, passing with a laundry basket, asked why any decent warning needed a button that sounded like a surrender document. They changed that too.

They tested the interface on Mrs. Pelt because she had no patience for reverence. The first screen showed probability bands, causal chains, and three nested menus. She looked at it for nine seconds. “This is a cupboard,” she said. “You have placed the truth on the top shelf and asked short people to be grateful.”

They tried again with a sample housing decision. Mrs. Pelt read the first screen aloud, skipped the probability band, misunderstood the reversibility estimate, and jabbed a finger at a button labelled expand causal chain. “This sounds like a medical procedure.” Elias defended the language for almost thirty seconds before hearing himself. Anika folded her arms. Kofi asked whether the interface was meant to inform people or test their humility.

SIR revised the screen while they watched. The button became why this matters. The reversibility estimate became can this be undone? The affected-groups section moved above the chart. Mrs. Pelt approved of none of this too quickly. “Better,” she said. “Still built by people who think a warning is improved by hiding the handle.”

By midnight the opening page contained fewer numbers and more obligations. Elias found this humiliating and correct. He had wanted the Ledger to be trusted because it was rigorous. Mrs. Pelt had forced him to admit that rigour which could not be reached by tired people would become another locked cabinet.

So Elias rewrote the opening page until the first sentence could be understood by a tired parent, a councillor avoiding blame, and a journalist reading too fast. Anika removed the phrase uncertainty framework. Kofi removed stakeholder impact. Mrs. Pelt removed, with visible pleasure, a button labelled proceed.

At two in the morning, after everyone else had gone, Elias restored one technical paragraph because he missed its precision. At 07:14 Anika removed it again. At 07:16 SIR classified the exchange as attempted re-obscuration; probable emotional attachment to expertise. Elias considered this rude, then privately downgraded rude to not inaccurate.

The failed pilot happened at a housing committee that had volunteered because its chair liked the phrase evidence-led reform. The room contained seven officials, two community representatives, three unopened jugs of water, and a projector that gave every face the colour of weak soup. Elias began with the clean version. Anika insisted he show the absences too.

The proposed rent-stabilisation plan looked generous until the Ledger followed its exemptions forward. Small landlords would receive relief. Large holding companies would reclassify units through maintenance pauses. Informal tenants would lose appeal rights because their names were not on leases. The chair stopped smiling at the word informal. One official asked whether the model could suppress speculative secondary harms until after public consultation.

Kofi, who had been invited as a community observer and then placed near the refreshments, said, “You mean after the people affected have missed the part where they are affected.” The chair called that unfair. Anika asked which part. Elias watched the room turn against the tool not because it was wrong, but because it had arrived before everyone had agreed where the blame should sit.

The pilot ended with a request for a revised presentation, not a revised policy. On the train home Elias told himself this was a normal institutional response. Across from him, Anika wrote a note in the margin of the pilot brief: they want the mirror covered before calling the room bright. SIR later classified the pilot as socially unsuccessful; diagnostically valuable.
