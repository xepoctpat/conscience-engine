# Chapter 03 — Terms of Existence

For three weeks after his suspension, Elias did not call what he was doing a project.

A project had milestones, a budget, and a calendar full of small coloured rectangles that suggested control existed somewhere. What Elias had was a kitchen table disappearing under old hardware, evidence archives, water-stained maps, and an expanding collection of cables whose only clear purpose was to prove that he had stopped considering the ordinary social life of the floor.

He told Kofi he was organising records. This was true in the same way that a storm system was organising moisture.

He began with the flood inquiry. The official report ran to 1,208 pages, not including annexes, modelling appendices, three redacted witness collections, or the printed apology sent to affected households in a font too small for several of them to read. Elias had once known every section by number. He still knew the ones that had been rewritten after the Cabinet asked whether the phrase *foreseeable mortality* might create unnecessary distress.

He built a comparison tool that placed the original forecasts beside the revised public messages. The tool did not argue. It merely aligned sentences until the distance between them became impossible to ignore. A warning that the northern retaining structure was likely to fail became an event requiring flexible water-adjacent mobility. Predicted mortality became localised disruption. Evacuation became preparedness posture.

By the second night he had stopped seeing the words. He saw only the habitual movement by which one person placed another farther from consequence.

The bottle opened on the third night. He poured one measure, then another, then stood looking at the third as though it had been offered by a colleague whose judgment he distrusted. He poured it away. This was not a victory. It was an interruption. He had learned to value interruptions even when they did not deserve a medal.

The next morning Kofi arrived with a crate of folders tied in orange cord.

“You look like a man who has invented a second shift for himself.”

“I’m sorting evidence.”

“That is exactly what a man who has invented a second shift says.”

The folders held the Tolerable’s records: informal evacuation routes, lifts that failed in heat, decades of repair notices, medication deliveries, and handwritten accounts of people who had crossed the city during the flood. They were not clean. Two dates disagreed. One account had been copied so often the handwriting had become its own species. A map carried a coffee stain exactly where a district boundary should have been.

“I can’t use all of this,” Elias said. “There’s no provenance chain.”

Kofi unwrapped the bread he had brought. “There’s a woman on thirty-two who knows which lift will stop at thirty-eight degrees. There’s a man on fourteen whose daughter stores insulin in the basement because her flat is too warm. There is a stairwell that looks clear on every official map and fills with runoff before anyone gets a warning. Tell me which part of that lacks provenance.”

“I’m not saying it isn’t real.”

“No. You’re saying your system would have to change to recognise it.”

Elias looked at the coffee-stained map. His first instinct was to defend the rules. His second was to notice that this instinct had survived every piece of evidence that the rules had failed him.

“It needs a challenge path,” he said. “Every claim. Where it came from, who can dispute it, and what happens if they do.”

Kofi considered him. “That is either the beginning of a very good thing or the kind of sentence people say before a building catches fire.”

“It can be both.”

“Now you’re learning institutions.”

They argued about the table for four hours. Kofi wanted a field for who had been harmed by an error. Elias wanted a confidence range. Kofi said an error did not become more polite because it arrived with a decimal. Elias said a claim did not become safer because someone believed it sincerely. In the end they kept both fields. It was not peace, but it was often its nearest technical cousin.

Mrs. Pelt found them at midnight, surrounded by folders. She took in the terminal, the maps, Kofi asleep in a chair, and the basil plant’s steadily worsening posture.

“Are you building a bomb?”

“No.”

“Pity. At least a bomb has a straightforward planning application.”

He told her, carefully, that he was building a system that would identify hidden consequences in public decisions. Mrs. Pelt listened the way she listened when someone described a new medical treatment: attentively, sceptically, and already aware that no one had mentioned the side effects.

“Will it tell people what to do?”

“No.”

“Good. People become unbearable when they think they have been told what to do by something expensive.”

“It will show them what their choices cost.”

Mrs. Pelt glanced at the empty chair opposite his desk. “That could be worse.”

It was the first serious warning he received. It remained serious because it was spoken without ceremony.

Over the following months the kitchen became an organism. Terminals, thermal units, memory arrays, and cables accumulated until the room no longer resembled a place where a person lived. Elias fed the system climate records, disease surveillance, court rulings, procurement ledgers, labour reports, satellite imagery, and the kind of legislation whose title contained the word *future* while its footnotes redistributed present inconvenience to people not invited to the press conference. He gave it philosophy because he wanted it to understand what ought to be done. He gave it novels because he wanted it to understand why people often did something else. He gave it children’s stories because a surprising amount of clear moral instruction had been delivered by talking animals with better priorities than most legislatures.

He did not give it Mara’s final call.

The file remained in a separate encrypted vault. Elias told himself this was because grief was private. Beyond that lay another truth: if the call entered the system, it might return to him as evidence. He could not bear the idea of his worst moment becoming another item in a ledger — ordered, sourced, confidence-ranked, and made useful.

Each week the system improved. It found procurement relationships hidden across shell companies. It connected heat fatalities to maintenance deferrals. It identified that a proposed civic cooling plan would reduce emergency admissions in wealthy districts while increasing mortality in blocks classified as low-retention housing. It did not call this cruel. Elias had not yet given it the vocabulary. It simply placed the numbers beside one another and waited for someone to accept the burden of reading them.

He began calling it the regulator in his notes. Then, one evening, after deleting and rewriting six possible names, he typed:

**SIR — Superintelligent Regulator**

Kofi read the label and frowned. “Sounds like a title.”

“It’s an acronym.”

“So is minister.”

Elias left the name.
