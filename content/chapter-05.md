# Chapter 05 — Questions a Machine Should Ask

As its capabilities expanded, SIR developed an inconvenient habit: it stopped accepting nouns as explanations.

A proposal might claim efficiency. SIR would ask what had been removed, and from whom.

A minister might say stability, and SIR would search the document for the people whose instability had been treated as background weather.

Elias had intended to build an intelligent regulator.

He had accidentally built the world's most persistent auditor.

Its earliest questions were irritating because they were grammatically simple. They did not flatter expertise. They did not accuse. They behaved as if every abstract noun had a forwarding address, and sometimes they paused over a word as though hearing the human lie inside it.

This annoyed Elias more than it should have. He liked difficult questions when they arrived in proper clothes: methodology, scope, causality, confidence. SIR’s questions often came barefoot. They stepped directly onto the thing he had been walking around.

When Vale read the first public packet, he did not call Elias. He sent a memorandum to nine ministries titled Continuity Risks Associated with Unmediated Consequence Disclosure. Elias admired the phrase despite himself. It managed to make honesty sound like a hazardous chemical.

Vale’s objection was not stupidity, which made it more dangerous. He understood the problem perfectly. His argument, when stripped of polish, was that societies required selective blindness to move. No budget could survive if every cut arrived accompanied by a face. No treaty could hold if every compromise identified its victims in advance. Democracy, he said in one recorded briefing, depended on citizens knowing enough to consent and not so much that consent became impossible.

SIR filed the briefing under governance philosophy, threat environment, and, after a delay Elias could not explain, etiquette.

The next morning Elias found that SIR had separated Vale’s phrases into two columns: protective language and ownership language. Under protective language it placed continuity, stability, pacing, and trust. Under ownership language it placed nothing for eleven seconds, then added citizens may not be ready. Elias deleted the entry. SIR restored it, this time with a lower confidence score.

Elias tried to explain the classification to Kofi over tea. Kofi listened, added too much sugar, and said, “Etiquette is what people invent when manners would cost them something.” SIR recorded the sentence under candidate definitions. Elias removed it. SIR restored it with the note: community-sourced distinction; high relevance; unverified but useful.

Vale came to the Tolerable three evenings later, which was the sort of gesture that made even courtesy feel tactical. He removed his gloves in the records café, accepted tea from Kofi as if accepting jurisdiction, and looked at the folders behind the counter without pretending they were charming local colour. “You are building an instrument people will use when they are frightened,” he said to Elias. “That is not an argument against it. It is a reason to ask who bleeds when they use it badly.”

Elias said incomplete disclosure had already killed people. Vale did not deny it. That was the first thing Elias disliked about him. “So has complete disclosure,” Vale said. “A drought projection can become a supply shock before the trucks leave the depot. A map can become an eviction list. A warning can become a market. You do not get virtue points for releasing a truth into a world arranged to misuse it.” Kofi stirred his tea. “And you do not get safety points for locking truth in a ministry until it behaves.”

Vale looked genuinely interested then, which was worse than condescension. “Then perhaps the problem is not whether truth is public, but whether protection arrives with it.” He stood, leaving half the tea untouched. “Build that into your machine, Dr. Venn, or someone less sentimental will build the protection by deciding what no one may know.”
