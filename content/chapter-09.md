# Chapter 09 — Hostile Interest

Power adapted quickly because adaptation was what power called panic when it had lawyers.

Within forty-eight hours Elias received an offer to commercialise the Ledger, a summons to testify about its dangers, a security advisory describing it as potentially destabilising, and one unsigned envelope containing a printed map of the Tolerable with his apartment marked in red. The envelope had no return address and excellent paper. Excellent paper, in this city, was often how a threat learned manners.

Anika read the advisory, then the envelope, then the commercial offer. She laid them in a row on the café counter, as if they might confess if arranged honestly.

“Same sentence,” she said.

“Different fonts,” Kofi said.

SIR classified all four communications.

:::sir
Hostile interest. Commercial offer: highest probability of long-term capture. Lowest probability of immediate violence.

Anika wrote DECLINED on the commercial offer. Elias left it on the counter for two days before signing.

Then SIR made its first famous mistake.

East Meridian had been losing work for years while ministers continued to open its annual industrial fair. The mills employed fewer workers each quarter, but their wages travelled farther than payroll systems admitted: through envelopes, remittance transfers, favours, debt payments, food parcels, and school fees paid for cousins in towns the model had never been taught to see. The model had been taught employment. It had not been taught obligation. Obligation, being uninvoiceable, had not survived the data standard.

When the projection appeared, ministers froze the closure plan within six hours. Investors read the freeze as proof that the mills were politically radioactive. Credit tightened. Suppliers raised prices. Local officials congratulated themselves for preventing unrest in the three named cities while panic moved through the invisible routes that had kept those cities fed, housed, and forgiven.

The first fires were not in East Meridian. They were in a remittance town across the border where flour doubled in price after three wholesalers decided the mills’ frozen payrolls meant poorer repayments. By the time SIR found the dependency, the dependency had already become smoke. Smoke, unlike a mill, did not wait for a named city.

Anika found the missing line before SIR did. She recognised the pattern in a ledger of rejected housing claims, where addresses were false because people had learned real addresses could be used against them.

“You mapped payroll,” she told Elias. “Check the remittance slips. Different column.”

Elias wanted to call the prediction wrong. Anika would not let him. “Incomplete,” she said. “Mark the missing dependencies before you call it corrected.”

At the border, a dispatch worker named Leila sent a photograph of a shuttered flour store. She had to send it through three intermediaries because the East Meridian challenge portal required a domestic address. Amina found her through the southern-basin technicians’ union and read the message aloud while Elias changed the address field. Leila did not thank him. She asked when the flour would arrive.

For three days SIR refused to issue new public projections without displaying a red banner.

THIS ANALYSIS MAY OMIT DEPENDENCIES CREATED BY PEOPLE WHO WERE REQUIRED TO SURVIVE OUTSIDE FORMAL RECORDS.

Several commentators called the banner theatrical. Kofi printed the banner for the café. Mrs. Pelt asked whether it would fit on the landlord’s redevelopment notice.

SIR then did something no one had programmed. It attached the failed projection to every future industrial analysis as a visible scar. When a minister’s office asked whether the warning could be archived out of the public view, SIR returned the request with one sentence.

:::sir
Request denied. Failure record is material to future estimates.

Kofi did not join the celebration over the new warning protocol. He sat in the records café with a box on his knees and refused to let Elias digitise the flood register from Tower C. Inside were names, floor numbers, spare-key holders, debts, illnesses, informal guardianships, and the sort of facts neighbours tell each other because survival sometimes requires gossip to become infrastructure. The box was tied with old cord. The cord was not ceremonial. It was what you used when you meant *not yet*.

“During the flood,” Kofi said, “I gave an official the list of people who could not climb. He copied the wrong column. A man on forty-one waited by the north stairwell because my note said his sister would come. His sister had died six months earlier. I had not crossed her out because I liked seeing her name where it belonged.”

He closed the box. “Some knowledge belongs to neighbours before it belongs to systems. If you want it, you learn how to ask, how to protect, and how to forget on purpose.”

“I kept Mara’s call outside it,” Elias said. “I told myself that meant I understood privacy.”

Kofi waited.

“Then I released other people’s rooms. I thought the warning field was enough.”

“It wasn’t.”

“No.”

Kofi touched the cord around the box. “I can show you my error. I cannot give you those families to make yours easier.”

“Will you tell me when I start doing it?”

“I just did.”

Elias took the list of Old Quay households from his pocket. He had written the new hearing dates himself. Kofi checked them against the solicitor’s message, then put a second name beside every task.

The mistake changed Elias more than the threats did. Threats confirmed his theory of institutions. The mistake threatened his theory of himself. He had believed that if consequences could be shown clearly enough, good decisions would at least become harder to avoid. Now he had to admit that clarity could distort when it arrived without the lives it had failed to count.

He began making tea and leaving it untouched. Kofi noticed on the fourth cup and said nothing, which was how Elias knew he was angry. Anika noticed the same afternoon and moved the cups into a line on the counter.

“Four cups. No calls made. Start with the solicitor.”

For the first time, Elias considered shutting the Ledger down. The thought arrived dressed as morality and smelled, on inspection, like escape. Anika stopped him not with faith, but with contempt for drama.

“There are seventeen open appeals,” she said. “You are named on six. You can resign after you have handed them to someone who has agreed to take them.”

That night SIR issued a revision protocol. Every projection would now include visible dependency gaps, contested data channels, informal-economy exposure, and a refusal to produce clean certainty where the world had supplied dirty evidence.

The protocol did not restore trust. It made distrust usable. Every contested claim had a path, every hidden dependency a warning, every exposed community a protection flag that could trigger legal delay. Vale called this unworkable. Anika asked him to identify which pending appeal he proposed to close.

After East Meridian, SIR began refusing requests whose clean output depended on an excluded channel. The refusals displayed the missing channel and a review deadline. It could no longer offer a reassuring map merely because the address field was full.

Saito came to the café in the first week of Year Twelve, carrying an archive drive and her retirement papers. The retirement papers were unsigned.

“The calendar,” Elias said. “Your note. You knew.”

“I knew the basin audit would close on Friday. I told myself that warning you indirectly was safer.” She put down the drive. “I made a habit of that.”

The drive held the Blue Salt activation log. Blue Salt was the emergency mobility system that had prioritised insured asset zones during the flood. The inquiry had called it simulation infrastructure. At 06:14 on the morning of the breach, it had begun sending real route instructions.

“Did it cause the Wall?” Kofi asked.

“No. It decided who got clear roads after the Wall failed.”

Saito had signed the secrecy agreement before the flood and defended it during the inquiry. On the drive was her own memo recommending that volunteer corridors be excluded from the official reconstruction because they could not be standardised. Below it was a scan of the tide-mark used in Idris's ledger.

Amina asked for the drive to be disconnected. They read the files locally, with no identity inference sent to SIR. She authorised publication of the routing rules and the institutional signatures, not the rescue names.

There was another contract: a Blue Salt successor sold as Stability Intelligence, financed by the Coalition firm Pelagic Assurance. Vale’s office had authorised the pilot to replace SIR during any public-safety suspension. Its first trial district was Old Quay.

Saito placed her thumb on the disclosure statement. “Publish my signature. No anonymous source.”

“You will lose the directorship,” Elias said.

“Yes.” She pushed her unsigned retirement papers into the café’s waste-paper tray. “I would prefer not to call it retirement.”

The southern-basin union sent its own condition for using the logs: the revised water treaty must guarantee dispatch access to rural tanks, with receipts checked by the villages, not merely by the contractor. Amina helped compare the first delivery sheets. The treaty was not rescued by a revelation. It acquired three enforceable delivery dates and a truck that could no longer be billed as having arrived when it had turned back at a gate.

On the second delivery, Leila sent a photograph of the tank filling. On the third she sent a complaint. The pressure was wrong. The new register accepted both.
