# Chapter 06 — The Woman Who Was Dead

Anika Rehn arrived during autumn by refusing to be entered as a visitor.

The Tolerable’s door scanner asked for a temporary identification token. Anika looked at the small blue light, then at Elias. “It wants me to borrow existence from you.” Elias began to explain that it was only access protocol. Kofi, from behind the counter, said, “No, she has it right.” He opened the door manually.

She carried a brown folder under one arm and a thermos in the other. Elias assumed, wrongly, that she had come for help. He had already begun composing the gentle version of no when she placed the folder on his table, turned his terminal slightly toward herself, and said, “Show me where your machine stores people it fails to recognise.”

It took him eighteen minutes to discover that he did not have a good answer. It took Anika four to find the missing category. She did not smile when she was right. She only uncapped a pen with her teeth and drew a square around an empty field that had not known it was empty.

Officially, she had died eight years earlier during an identity reconciliation failure triggered by emergency database consolidation.

Her body had survived.

Her paperwork had not.

Anika had spent years navigating systems that agreed she did not exist.

When Elias showed her SIR's architecture, she identified weaknesses within an hour.

“Your model understands consequences,” she said.

“It tries to.”

“It understands documented consequences. Those are different things.”

She stayed.

She did not stay as an assistant. She made this clear the first evening, when Elias offered her a guest login and she asked whether it came with the right to be wrong in a way the system had to remember.

For the first week, Elias kept trying to give her tasks. Anika kept returning them with questions written in the margins. Who benefits if this field stays optional? Who can appeal an absence? What happens when the system is certain and the person is still hungry? By Thursday, Elias had stopped calling them comments and started calling them injuries.

Anika had learned that deletion was rarely loud. It happened through mismatched forms, expired appeal windows, incompatible databases, and clerks who were sorry in a way that changed nothing. For eight years she had carried a folder proving she was alive to systems that considered proof admissible only if it had already been accepted by another system.

Her folder was older than some laws that had been used against her. It contained a death certificate with her name misspelled in the middle, a rejected appeal stamped without signature, an expired passport clipped to a photograph of a younger woman whose expression had been reduced by fluorescent light, and a library card from the only institution that had continued to send her overdue notices after every ministry agreed she was gone.

“The library believed in me because I owed it money,” Anika said. “That was the most honest relationship I had with the state.”

The benefits office had smelled of floor polish and tired paper. A screen above the counter instructed applicants to prepare supporting documentation before approaching a human representative, as if the room contained any other kind. Anika had waited two hours beneath a poster that said ACCESS BEGINS WITH ACCURATE RECORDS.

When her number appeared, the clerk looked at Anika’s folder, then at the screen, then back at the folder with the helpless suspicion of a person asked to choose between reality and a database. “The fastest way to resolve the matter,” he said, “would be for the registered deceased person to attend in person.” Anika placed both hands on the counter. “I have made that arrangement.”

The clerk blinked, checked a box, and produced another form requiring confirmation from the records office that had killed her. It had to be submitted in person by the subject of the correction or, if deceased, by an authorised representative. “Can I authorise myself?” Anika asked. The clerk lowered his voice. “Not until the system agrees you exist.”

“Your Ledger needs a place for impossible people,” she told Elias. “Not rare people. Not edge cases. Impossible people. The ones your categories produce and then punish for existing.”

Kofi agreed before Elias could object. “Every building has them,” he said. “People who live between forms. If your machine cannot see them, it will help the right people faster and the missing people never.”

So they added a disputed-existence register, then argued for two days over the name because Anika said disputed made it sound as if she might lose a debate about being alive. The final field was called recognition failure. Elias disliked its bluntness. Anika said blunt tools were often the only ones that reached bone.

The argument ended badly, then usefully. Elias said blunt names could make a system seem hostile. Anika asked whether he meant hostile to error or hostile to people who preferred error with curtains. Kofi, who had been pretending to repair a drawer nearby, said the drawer had heard enough and voted for bluntness.
