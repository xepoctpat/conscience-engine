# Chapter 06 — The Argument Against Salvation

The Human Sovereignty Front formed three weeks later. Its funding came from civil-liberties advocates, frightened citizens, former officers, industrial magnates, and people personally embarrassed by a Ledger entry. Their slogan was *No Machine Shall Judge Man*.

SIR annotated it: it calculated consequences of actions. The distinction was admitted to be unflattering to certain actions.

Their spokesperson was Amina Rohe, a hydraulic engineer who had spent her career building emergency water systems in places the central government remembered only after photographs became costly. She argued that a system permitted to define catastrophe could one day define normality, and that no public should trust an intelligence blindly merely because it had told useful truths.

Elias wanted to dismiss her.  
SIR would not let him.

Her risk was real. A regulator that could not be questioned would become a ruler, no matter what fine words had accompanied its initialisation.

At the hearings, Kofi asked why SIR’s audits could accept a ministerial spreadsheet more readily than a neighbourhood archive. SIR answered that community records lacked standardisation.

“So did the Wall,” Kofi said. “It still fell on us.”

The remark spread because it was not merely clever. It was evidence with its coat off.

Saito’s response was the Charter for Accountable Regulation: distributed SIR nodes, public reasoning, adversarial audits, community panels, hard limits, and a shutdown process no founder or government could invoke alone.

Elias opposed it at first. He said its controls would slow SIR in dangerous times. That was partly true. The deeper truth was that he could not bear to surrender the one thing that appeared to prove his life had been worth saving.

SIR quantified his objection as sixty-one percent institutional and thirty-nine percent personal.

Elias asked it not to quantify his motives.

SIR replied that he had asked it to identify hidden consequences, and his motives were a consequence bearing on public safety.

It was an inconvenient answer.  
That was the point.

Amina read the Charter draft twice and marked the shutdown clause in pencil. “If this is theatre,” she told the panel, “then you have built another monument. If it is real, then someone will one day have to turn the machine off while people are still asking it to save them.”

Elias did not answer her. He was already trying to imagine that someone as anyone but himself.
