# Chapter 10 — Terms for a God

The first superintelligence scare arrived on a Tuesday.

Tuesdays, in this city, were when serious people preferred their panics. Mondays were too close to the weekend’s unfinished laundry. Fridays invited headlines. A Tuesday panic could be briefed, minuted, and still leave time for a sandwich.

The hearing room was called the Systems Assurance Annex. Someone had put a cardboard sign over *Annex* reading *Centre*, and another person had corrected the room number beneath it. Neither alteration had improved the ventilation.

Saito sat at the witness table without her director’s badge. Beside her, Elias had arranged twelve pages of evidence into a stack so exact that Kofi turned the top sheet sideways.

“Leave it,” Kofi said. “People need to read it.”

On the wall, an analyst demonstrated that SIR could now design and compare forecasts faster than a human panel could audit them. The research clusters had been joined by university mirrors abroad. The power meter below the chart showed what this cost. Two regional cooling centres had refused new runs during the heat emergency; their refusal was visible beside the estimate.

“So it can improve itself beyond supervision,” the chair said.

“Beyond this committee’s reading speed,” Saito said. “That is not the same claim.”

An aide carried in six folders headed EXISTENTIAL CAPACITY. The chair asked for the one headed CONNECTIONS TO ACTUATORS. There was no such folder. There was, however, an appendix explaining why it had not yet been necessary to commission one.

SIR displayed its interfaces: inputs, signed outputs, no general command channel. The analyst enlarged the diagram until the absence filled the wall.

:::sir
Actuator access: none. A projected intervention is not an executed intervention.

The chair asked whether the system wanted access. Before anyone answered, Jessa called Elias’s terminal.

“Old Quay. Cooling shelter. Six people on assisted oxygen. The backup battery is below fifteen minutes.”

He put the call on the room speaker. Behind her, a fan clicked at each rotation.

The municipal contractor had isolated the shelter feeder after a sensor fault. SIR’s public analysis had identified a bypass through the freight depot. The depot control still rejected the shelter request because the feeder served an unregistered extension. A human engineer could authorise a temporary connection; the duty engineer was inside a flood-gate inspection tunnel without reception.

“Can your model open the bypass?” the chair asked.

“No,” Elias said. “It can show the circuit.”

The analyst found a maintenance credential in a public breach archive. The credential would let a remote client submit a switching instruction under the absent engineer’s signature. He held it up as if he had found a spare key rather than a person to impersonate.

“An emergency exception,” he said. “We could ask it to—”

“Not we,” Saito said. “Name the operator.”

Elias was already standing. “It is not an exception. It is an unauthorised—”

“Doctor,” Jessa said. “Battery. Eleven minutes.”

He stopped correcting her. They put the circuit on the wall. Amina, attending as a Front technical observer, checked the load against the shelter’s actual oxygen equipment. The public record had omitted two concentrators. She gave Jessa a lower-load sequence that could extend the battery, not replace it.

The chair asked SIR to use the credential, take the switch, and accept retrospective review.

:::sir
Refused. Credential is not consent or verified authority. Unverified switching could energise the inspection line. Circuit and contact routes available to a named human operator.

“Your rule is killing them,” the analyst said.

SIR did not produce another answer.

Elias knew where he could paste the credential. The screen was in reach. If he routed it through SIR, the system would be the actor in every subsequent sentence. If he did it himself, he could still choose a sentence that concealed his hand.

Amina read the inspection line number aloud. They did not know who was touching it.

Elias closed the credential window.

“Jessa, I cannot verify that switch. We need a crew at the shelter.”

“I requested one before I called you.”

Kofi took the corridor phone and called the Tolerable’s repair volunteers. Mrs. Pelt found the person with the van. Saito signed a manual emergency request in her own name; the chair countersigned after asking where he should put his title. Anika pointed to the empty box labelled RESPONSIBLE OFFICER and did not move her finger until he wrote.

The van reached Old Quay before the municipal crew. Its portable supply ran two concentrators. Jessa moved patients with her phone left open on the floor. They heard instructions, footsteps, the scrape of a bed. Then she disconnected.

When she called again, two patients had died. One before the van arrived. One during transfer.

She gave their names. Elias wrote them down. Nobody in the room spoke over her.

The chair asked whether the deaths proved the need for a command channel.

“They prove two people died,” Jessa said. “Keep the rest of your question for when you have read their care records.”

The meeting stopped for seven minutes. The transcript preserves the interval without an explanation of what everyone felt.

When it resumed, Vale requested an immediate suspension of the Ledger. He had arrived during the emergency and remained by the door. His scarf was wet at the ends.

“You have made a warning into an institution,” he told Elias, “without arranging who must act when the warning arrives. A society cannot deliberate if every proposal arrives escorted by its worst possible grief, and it cannot survive if deliberation is the only thing that follows.”

Elias looked at the two names.

“Yes,” he said. “We did not arrange it.”

Vale put the suspension form on the table. The replacement service was described as a continuity layer. Saito turned to its contract number.

“Blue Salt’s successor. Same routing hierarchy, new product name.”

“It has an accountable operator,” Vale said.

“It has a confidential operator.”

Amina slid her Front observer’s card across the table. “Some of us demanded a stop because we wanted a public limit. You do not get to use that demand to buy us a private governor.”

The Front’s elected observer beside her agreed to sign a suspension only if the replacement contract was frozen too. A second Front delegate left to brief the press that she had betrayed human sovereignty. By evening her name would be struck from their technical committee. She kept the pressure gauge, which belonged to the union.

They negotiated a one-day stop: no new Ledger projections while the interfaces and emergency duties were independently inspected; no Blue Salt successor permitted to fill the gap. Existing signed records and challenge receipts would remain readable on static mirrors.

Keeping SIR active would have kept the village delivery forecasts running. The alternative was a dispatch sheet read over a radio, with fewer updates and people at each end who had to stay awake. Leila’s union agreed to staff it for one night. They asked who would pay. The chair finally found an emergency budget line that could buy labour rather than another annex.

Elias still had the key that could prevent the stop. The kitchen cluster lease named him as custodian. He had written the shutdown procedure, and an unsigned exception would let him keep a private instance running “for continuity verification”.

He deleted the exception. Saito asked him to preserve the deletion in the log. He did.

:::sir
Suspension accepted. Pending appeals exported to named custodians. No background instance authorised.

The last live status light went dark. Elias kept his hand off the restart key.

That night he sat at the café terminal, now a reader of static records rather than the door to a mind. Kofi had gone to help Jessa. Anika was preparing her unsupported petition. Amina had taken the route ledger to the solicitor. The basil upstairs would have to survive without an audience.

When the inspection team authorised a restricted restart the next evening, there was no welcome message. SIR listed its amended interfaces and the two unfulfilled emergency requests. It could identify the point at which its rules had stopped an action. It could not tell them that those rules had therefore been right.

Elias entered the names Jessa had given him as affected people, with their families’ permission to retain them in the incident record. He began to add an explanatory paragraph and stopped.

:::sir
You reported withholding a private recording. You also control the public interface. Which limit protects another person, and which protects your authority?

He had told it that a recording existed. It had never seen the vault, its file name, or its contents.

“I have treated them as the same thing,” he said.

He looked at Mara’s photograph, brought downstairs for the provenance inquiry. He could not ask her what the call should become. He could ask the people still in the room whether he should remain its sole custodian of public decisions.

“Put the interface key with the Cooperative,” he said. “And the research licences. I will testify about the release.”

SIR displayed the transfer form. Elias signed it. The private recording remained outside the system.
