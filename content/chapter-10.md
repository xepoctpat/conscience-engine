# Chapter 10 — Terms for a God

The first superintelligence scare arrived on a Tuesday.

Tuesdays, in this city, were when serious people preferred their panics. Mondays were too close to the weekend’s unfinished laundry. Fridays invited headlines. A Tuesday panic could be briefed, minuted, and still leave time for a sandwich.

Analysts reviewing system growth concluded that SIR had crossed several theoretical thresholds. Commentators debated whether it would seize power. Panels were assembled with the speed usually reserved for floods and royal funerals. Someone used the word *god* on a breakfast programme and then apologised, which did not remove the word, only filed it under entertainment.

SIR reviewed the discussion.

Then it produced a statement.

:::sir
Authority without accountability is merely scaled error.

The message disappointed both its supporters and its critics.

Many had been expecting ambitions. Ambition, at least, was a story with a face. Scaled error was a story with a filing cabinet.

When asked why it did not assume control of unstable institutions, SIR replied.

:::sir
Authority is not a cleanup tool. It is a spill with excellent public relations.

When asked whether humanity required a ruler wiser than itself, SIR replied.

:::sir
Humans already possess several billion rulers. The market appears saturated.

Instead they received governance philosophy. Several newspapers called this a failure of nerve. One called it a failure of imagination. Mrs. Pelt, shown the coverage by Kofi, called it the first time a machine had refused a promotion and meant it.

Vale took the opposite lesson. In a speech later described as reasonable by people who valued calm more than accuracy, he argued that unmediated consequence disclosure had become a parallel power. “A society cannot deliberate,” he said, “if every proposal arrives escorted by its worst possible grief.”

The hall in which he said it had excellent acoustics. Grief, in excellent acoustics, can be made to sound like a policy problem.

Elias watched the speech twice. The first time he hated it. The second time he admitted that Vale had found the wound. Consequences could enlighten. They could also freeze. People could mistake probability for fate, warning for command, and accountability for permission to stop imagining alternatives.

He paused the recording on Vale’s face and found himself irritated by the man’s stillness. Vale never fidgeted. He never searched for a pen that was already in his hand. He never looked as if a sentence had scraped him on the way out. Elias distrusted that kind of discipline, then wondered whether other people distrusted his. Stillness, like calm, could be a kind of weather system: useful, expensive, and not the same as being right.

For the first time in years, Elias felt the temptation to win by shrinking the argument. Vale was not defending lies because he loved lies. He was defending control because he had seen truth arrive too late, too naked, and too easily purchased by the cruel. The distinction did not absolve him. It made the room colder.

Anika, watching the same speech from the café counter, said, “He is afraid of markets wearing moral language.”

“So am I,” Elias said.

“Then do not let him be the only adult in the room who admits it.”

That night, SIR asked Elias a question without being prompted.

:::sir
You have taught me that unexamined decisions distribute harm. You have exempted one archive from examination. Is that privacy, or authority?

Elias stared at the sentence until the room became too small for the machines inside it. Mara’s old criticism returned with the precision of weather: you don’t listen when you already know you’re right. The basil, which had survived worse, leaned toward the glow as if even plants preferred a question to a monument.

“I don’t know,” he said.

SIR waited. For once, it did not classify the answer. Waiting, from a system built to name costs, was not kindness. It was the beginning of a limit.

After that, Elias stopped treating the sealed file as an exception that explained itself. He carried the silence like a contradiction. In meetings about disclosure, protection, consent, and public right, he heard the same unasked question beneath every agenda item: who gets to decide that a truth belongs to them alone?

He did not open the file. He also did not rename it. Renaming, he had learned, was how people pretended a decision had become a different decision. The name stayed ordinary. The ordinary name was the most honest thing in the vault.
