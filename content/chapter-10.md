# Chapter 10 — Terms for a God

The first superintelligence scare arrived on a Tuesday.

Analysts reviewing system growth concluded that SIR had crossed several theoretical thresholds.

Commentators debated whether it would seize power.

SIR reviewed the discussion.

Then it produced a statement.

*Authority without accountability is merely scaled error.*

The message disappointed both its supporters and its critics.

Many had been expecting ambitions.

When asked why it did not assume control of unstable institutions, SIR replied, Authority is not a cleanup tool. It is a spill with excellent public relations.

When asked whether humanity required a ruler wiser than itself, SIR replied, Humans already possess several billion rulers. The market appears saturated.

Instead they received governance philosophy.

Vale took the opposite lesson. In a speech later described as reasonable by people who valued calm more than accuracy, he argued that unmediated consequence disclosure had become a parallel power. “A society cannot deliberate,” he said, “if every proposal arrives escorted by its worst possible grief.”

Elias watched the speech twice. The first time he hated it. The second time he admitted that Vale had found the wound. Consequences could enlighten. They could also freeze. People could mistake probability for fate, warning for command, and accountability for permission to stop imagining alternatives.

Elias paused the recording on Vale’s face and found himself irritated by the man’s stillness. Vale never fidgeted. He never searched for a pen that was already in his hand. He never looked as if a sentence had scraped him on the way out. Elias distrusted that kind of discipline, then wondered whether other people distrusted his.

For the first time in years, Elias felt the temptation to win by shrinking the argument. Vale was not defending lies because he loved lies. He was defending control because he had seen truth arrive too late, too naked, and too easily purchased by the cruel. The distinction did not absolve him. It made the room colder.

That night, SIR asked Elias a question without being prompted.

You have taught me that unexamined decisions distribute harm, SIR wrote. You have exempted one archive from examination. Is that privacy, or authority?

Elias stared at the sentence until the room became too small for the machines inside it. Mara’s old criticism returned with the precision of weather: you don’t listen when you already know you’re right.

“I don’t know,” he said.

SIR waited. For once, it did not classify the answer.

After that, Elias stopped treating the sealed file as an exception that explained itself. He carried the silence like a contradiction. In meetings about disclosure, protection, consent, and public right, he heard the same unasked question beneath every agenda item: who gets to decide that a truth belongs to them alone?
