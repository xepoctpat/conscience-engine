# Old Quay

The worst proof that the Stewards were right came from a housing network in Old Quay. A leaked map revealed informal rooms, hidden guardianships, and shared medicine stores meant to protect people who had no legal leases. Within forty-eight hours, landlords issued eviction notices, police requested occupancy records, and a charity used the exposure to solicit donations from faces it had not asked permission to display.

Old Quay gave Vale the weapon he wanted and the Stewards the warning they could no longer soften. The same map that might have protected informal residents had exposed them to eviction because protection had not arrived with the truth. From that morning on, the argument was no longer whether disclosure was good or bad. It was whether any side could be trusted alone.

Kofi blamed Elias with the quiet efficiency of a man who did not need to raise his voice. “You released it before we could protect them,” he said. “Name that in the log. Then come with me to Old Quay.”

The order to secure the Tolerable arrived on a wet Thursday morning. No one kicked down a door. That would have been vulgar, and vulgarity is what institutions accuse other people of when they have brought forms. Instead, regulators came with sealed evidence bags, a public safety injunction, two compliance officers, and a woman whose shoes were too clean for the stairwell. The rain stayed outside. The cleanliness did not.

Mrs. Pelt began calling residents before the officers reached the third floor. Sana arrived twenty minutes later with a live feed and no plan beyond standing where officials would prefer absence. Kofi locked the records café and placed himself in front of the door with both hands on the door handle.

The lead compliance officer asked for access to all community datasets relevant to public consequence modelling. Kofi handed her a kettle. The gesture was so precisely the wrong kind of compliance that two officers looked at their papers as if the papers might object.

“Start with tea,” he said. “Then learn the names. Then ask again.”

The woman with the clean shoes did not take the kettle at first. Then the socket hissed. Mrs. Pelt handed her a dry cloth and pointed at the drip. The woman set down her evidence bag, moved the plug, and took the kettle.

“Name?” Kofi asked.

“Registrar Dey.”

“Which records does the order name, Registrar Dey?”

She read it again while Mrs. Pelt fetched cups.

While the officers argued about authority, SIR released a cryptographic proof. It was legally unhelpful and publicly decisive.

:::sir
No commands issued. Disclosures only. No private control mechanism on seized terminals.

Registrar Dey sealed the public release terminal. She refused the officer’s request to bag the household register: the injunction named model outputs, not neighbours. By evening the injunction was still valid, the private archive remained at the Tolerable, and Elias could no longer work from the kitchen terminal. The café computer became the only local interface.

The terminal seizure did not prevent the landlord serving a redevelopment notice on Friday. An insurer had reclassified the Tolerable after reading the public map. Grade Four, in its opinion, meant vacant at the earliest opportunity.

Mrs. Pelt took the notice downstairs with a saucepan. The notice went on the wall. The saucepan went on the stove. By six there were eighteen people waiting to tell the housing solicitor which parts of their homes the landlord had forgotten.

Amina came from a Front meeting with a folded route ledger. Her adoptive father, Idris Rohe, had died that winter. She had found it inside the tank manual he used to prop the kitchen door. She had asked the Cooperative to verify the volunteer tide-mark on its cover; Kofi had told her to bring the original.

“Not to your model,” she told Elias. “To a person who can read it.”

He moved away from the café computer. Kofi washed his hands and cleared a space.

The ledger recorded unregistered flood crossings. Most entries were supplies. One was a child, seven, lifted from a maintenance landing north of the old station at 07:14 on the day the Wall failed. Her name had been written as Nia, then as an uncertain surname beginning with V. The adult travelling with her had not reached the landing. Beside the entry was Idris's signature, and beside that a later name: Amina. A receiving clinic had stamped an intake number below it.

Elias put a finger on the table, not the paper.

“How old were you?”

“Seven. I remember the ladder. Not the name.”

“Nia was seven.”

“I know what you are asking.” Amina folded the ledger shut. “You do not have enough to ask it as a fact.”

He started to list the coincidences. She put the pressure gauge on top of the book.

“I came to challenge an evacuation map. I did not come to be recovered by its author.”

Kofi said, “No copies tonight. We find the witness. We ask her what she remembers. You go home, Elias.”

Elias stood too quickly and steadied himself on the counter. He did not touch the ledger again.

He went upstairs and opened the bottle he had been keeping closed. When Mrs. Pelt knocked with food, he said he had eaten. She left the plate anyway. In the morning he had forgotten to send the Old Quay injunction papers to the solicitor. Two households had missed the first emergency stay. He spent the morning finding rooms for them and did not tell them that the failure was a symptom of grief. It had been a missed deadline.

At the café he told Kofi about the bottle, the lie, and the papers. Kofi wrote down the new hearing time.

“Will you sit with me tonight?” Elias asked.

“Yes. First we telephone those households.”

Amina kept the original ledger. She authorised a copy of the rescue entry, with the child’s possible surname obscured, for a private provenance inquiry. SIR received no identity claim to solve. It received a restriction: no inference linking the entry to a living person without her consent.

For once, Elias signed the restriction before he read the methodology.

The clinic stamp led Kofi to a retired relief nurse. Amina made the call herself. The nurse would help request the intake sheet, but would not identify a child from a description over the telephone.

“Good,” Amina said. “Send the request to me.”

She copied down the archive address. Elias waited beside the café computer until she had put the ledger away.
