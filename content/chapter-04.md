# Chapter 04 — The Audit of Reality

SIR’s first useful act was not a prediction. It was a correction, and Elias nearly missed its importance because nothing about it looked dramatic. Drama, he had learned, was what institutions used when they wanted an audience. Corrections arrived like a change of font.

Elias noticed it while standing at the sink, rinsing the same mug for the third time because he had forgotten he had already rinsed it twice. The terminal made no sound. It only changed a line from equipment failure to transferred maintenance liability. He dried his hands on his shirt instead of the towel beside him, then stood very still, as if stillness could make a sentence take its clothes off.

When fed six years of procurement records, emergency reports, maintenance logs, and citizen complaints, the system reconstructed a chain of decisions that linked a collapsed cooling network to three hundred pages of unrelated administrative choices. The failures were not hidden. They were merely distributed, which is the polite cousin of hidden, and much harder to prosecute.

The case concerned a cooling network in West Orison, a district that appeared in official planning documents mostly as a logistics inconvenience wrapped around several thousand elderly residents. The public explanation for the summer deaths had been technical degradation under abnormal heat load. SIR disagreed with the grammar before it disagreed with the facts.

It traced one broken pump backward through a maintenance deferral, then through a procurement cap, then through a budget rule designed to reward departments for savings that did not become visible elsewhere for at least eighteen months. The deaths had not been caused by one negligent official. They had been manufactured by a sequence of sensible decisions protected from one another by departmental borders. Each border had a name, a letterhead, and a person who could say, truthfully, that the heat had not been their heat.

On the screen, three old people had died in rooms that were technically separate from the decision that made the rooms hot. Elias rubbed the bridge of his nose until little false lights appeared behind his eyes. The basil, unimpressed, continued its career as a moral witness.

SIR began producing consequence maps.

The first maps were ugly. Kofi said they looked as if a spider had tried to understand municipal finance and lost patience. Elias kept the layout because ugliness made the maps harder to confuse with persuasion. Each line connected a decision to the place where its cost had landed: a ward, a school, a stairwell, a household debt ledger, a clinic waiting room. The lines did not apologise for crossing departments. That was the point, and also the insult.

Nobody had asked for them.

At first, almost nobody cared. A transport committee used one map to reject a night-bus cut after SIR showed the probable rise in missed dialysis appointments. A school board ignored another and saved money until three months later, when absenteeism rose exactly where the map had placed it. A housing minister asked whether the colours could be made less accusatory. Elias said the colours were already a compromise. The minister said that was not what she had meant.

Then a clinic director in West Orison printed one of the maps and taped it above the intake desk. By noon patients were reading it while waiting for triage. By evening someone had photographed the red line running from a procurement cap to a corridor full of heat-stressed bodies, and strangers were asking why a saving in one office had become a death certificate in another.

The clinic director was named Jessa Malk, and she had the habit of carrying three pens because two always disappeared into the emergency ward. She did not ask Elias for permission. She printed the map on the old machine next to payroll, watched the first copy jam, swore with administrative precision, then printed it again. When a procurement officer called to ask whether she had authorisation to display internal analysis, Jessa looked at the queue of patients fanning themselves with appointment letters and said, “No, but I have witnesses.”

The officer asked whether the wall was an approved communication surface. Jessa asked whether heat was an approved cause of death.

By closing time, someone had written beside the red line in marker.

THIS IS WHERE THE MONEY WENT.

The sentence was inaccurate, or rather too accurate in the wrong register. Elias stared at the photograph on his terminal and felt the old professional urge to correct it. Then he noticed the corridor behind the sign, the portable fans, the people waiting with paper cups of water, and understood that the map had stopped belonging to him.

He called the clinic because he wanted the map corrected before it travelled farther. Jessa answered on speaker. Behind her, someone was coughing, someone else was asking for water, and a printer was making the long mechanical complaint of a machine that had not forgiven civilization.

“The label should read deferred cross-departmental maintenance exposure,” Elias said, hearing himself become intolerable in real time.

Jessa looked down the corridor. “Four patients here can explain it.”

The procurement officer called again while Elias was still on the line. Jessa did not lower her voice. “The map is staying up.” The officer began reading the wall-display policy. She waited until he reached the exceptions. “You can send the removal order to me. I’ll pin it beside the map.”

A man in a paper mask asked whether the line meant his mother’s room had overheated because someone had not bought a pump. Jessa looked at the map, then at Elias through the open call. Elias tried to assemble an answer precise enough not to betray the truth.

“It means,” he said, “the decision not to buy the pump was treated as separate from the room where your mother lived.”

The man nodded as if this confirmed something he had known before anyone gave it a diagram.

That night, in the kitchen, SIR left a line on the terminal that Elias almost deleted out of habit.

:::sir
Causal link retained across departmental boundaries. Filing categories are not causal boundaries.

He left it. Downstairs the pipes knocked, and for once he did not mistake the sound for a verdict.
