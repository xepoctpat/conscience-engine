# Prologue: On the Defects of Simple Stories

Simple stories are told about complicated disasters because complicated disasters are inconvenient. They refuse to finish cleanly and have the poor manners to keep producing consequences after the official inquiry has gone home. An inquiry likes a date, a villain, a lesson, and a buffet. A disaster prefers to go on living in the stairwells.

The simple version says that Elias Venn built a machine, the machine saved the world, and everyone learned a valuable lesson in time for the commemorative plaque. This version is popular with curricula, ministries, and people who prefer their history pre-digested. It travels well because it asks almost nothing of the living except applause at the correct moment.

The other simple version says that Elias Venn built a machine, the machine nearly ruled the world, and wise officials stopped it at the last possible moment. This version remains useful to officials who were looking the other way while the danger sat in their own offices, wearing a visitor badge. It has the additional advantage of making panic look like statesmanship.

Neither version is accurate.

Accuracy, in this city, has always been treated as a kind of weather: acknowledged, resented, and planned around rather than invited indoors. The true account contains a flood, a file that should have been opened sooner, a woman who was legally dead and morally unimpressed, a neighbourhood archivist who believed that data required manners, and a superintelligence that declined the traditional career path of becoming a god. Its founder had taught it, rather carefully, that gods were mostly just rulers with better stationery.

This is not a confession. Confessions are for people who still hope to be forgiven in the same room where they sinned. This is a recovered civic record. Presentation has been separated from the evidence. Some names are here because they insisted. Some absences are here because they would not stay politely offstage.

What follows is not complete. Completeness is a word used by people who have not yet been asked what they omitted. The omissions that remain are marked where they can be marked. Where they cannot, the gap is left visible. That is not humility. It is procedure.

This record begins with the Northern Sea Wall, which is where the simpler versions prefer to start, and because they are not entirely wrong.

The Wall held for exactly as long as it had been funded to hold, and then performed the unforgivable act of meeting the sea.
